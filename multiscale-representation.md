# The Gaussian Kernel's Multiscale Representation in $\mathbb{R}^3$

The Gaussian kernel is one of the most useful tools in approximation theory and scientific computing because it encodes a continuum of scales in a single, analytically tractable family. In three dimensions, its properties make it a natural language for describing functions that have structure at many resolutions at once.

## The kernel and its scale parameter

In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the Gaussian kernel with width $\sigma > 0$ is

$$
G_\sigma(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{3/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{|\mathbf{x}|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$

It is normalized to unit mass, radially symmetric, and infinitely differentiable. Its Fourier transform is again a Gaussian, $\widehat{G_\sigma}(\boldsymbol{\xi}) = \exp(-\sigma^2|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^2/2)$, so it is localized in both space and frequency. This joint localization is optimal in the Heisenberg sense, which is why Gaussians are the natural building blocks for representing a function at a chosen resolution.

The parameter $\sigma$ is the scale. A small $\sigma$ resolves fine detail and a large $\sigma$ captures only coarse behavior. Representing a function at many values of $\sigma$ simultaneously is what gives the representation its multiscale character.

## The scale-space semigroup

The Gaussians form a semigroup under convolution:

$$
G_\sigma * G_\tau = G_{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\tau^2}}.
$$

This means that blurring at scale $\tau$ and then at scale $\sigma$ is the same as blurring once at the combined scale. The family is therefore self-consistent across scales: coarser representations are exact smoothings of finer ones, not independent approximations. Given $f$, the scale-space family

$$
u(\mathbf{x}, t) = (G_{\sqrt{2t}} * f)(\mathbf{x})
$$

solves the heat equation $\partial_t u = \Delta u$ with initial data $f$. Time $t$ plays the role of scale, and the representation at any scale is generated by diffusion from the finer one. Witkin, Koenderink, and Lindeberg showed that, under reasonable axioms such as causality, translation and rotation invariance, and the semigroup property, the Gaussian is essentially the only kernel that generates a scale space without creating new spurious structure as the scale increases.

## Approximation rates

The quality of a single-scale approximation $f_\sigma = G_\sigma * f$ is controlled by smoothness. In Fourier space, the multiplier is $1 - \exp(-\sigma^2|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^2/2)$, which is bounded by $\min(1, \sigma^2|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^2/2)$. For smooth $f$, a Taylor expansion gives

$$
f_\sigma - f = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Delta f + O(\sigma^4),
$$

so the error is second-order in the scale. More generally, for $f$ in a Sobolev space $H^s(\mathbb{R}^3)$ with $0 < s \le 2$, the $L^2$ error satisfies $\|f - f_\sigma\|_{L^2} \le C\,\sigma^s\,\|f\|_{H^s}$. The representation thus degrades gracefully: the rate of approximation is exactly the regularity of the function, and the scale parameter trades resolution for accuracy in a quantifiable way.

Because $f_\sigma$ is an analytic function of $\mathbf{x}$ for every $\sigma > 0$, the approximation is smooth at all scales, even when $f$ itself is rough. The errors are controlled, and the smoothing is applied uniformly.

## Multiscale decomposition and differences of Gaussians

The real power of the representation comes from combining scales. Define the detail at scale $k$ as the difference $D_k = f_{\sigma_k} - f_{\sigma_{k+1}}$, often called a difference of Gaussians (DoG). Then a telescoping sum gives

$$
f = f_{\sigma_L} + \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} D_k,
$$

an exact decomposition in which each $D_k$ isolates structure in a band of frequencies. This is the continuous analogue of the Laplacian pyramid of Burt and Adelson, and it has a wavelet-like structure without requiring orthogonal wavelets. The coarse term $f_{\sigma_L}$ captures global shape, and the detail terms add progressively finer features. Truncating the sum yields a controllable approximation: stopping at level $K$ leaves an error that is exactly the detail you chose to omit.

In $\mathbb{R}^3$ this decomposition is especially efficient because $G_\sigma$ is a product of one-dimensional Gaussians. Convolution can therefore be applied one axis at a time, reducing the cost of smoothing a grid of $N^3$ samples to $O(N^3)$ per level, independent of the kernel width.

## Gaussian radial basis expansions

A second route is to represent $f$ directly as a superposition of Gaussians placed at different centers and widths:

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) \approx \sum_{\ell}\sum_{j} c_{\ell j}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell j}|^2}{2\sigma_\ell^2}\right).
$$

A hierarchical scheme uses coarse centers with wide widths for global structure and dense centers with narrow widths for local detail, typically with $\sigma_\ell$ halving at each level. Such expansions are used in mesh-free solvers, surface reconstruction from scattered data, and modern volumetric rendering. Gaussian splatting, which represents a 3D scene as a cloud of anisotropic Gaussians, is a contemporary example of this idea.

The theoretical foundation is solid. The Gaussian kernel is strictly positive definite and its reproducing kernel Hilbert space is dense in the continuous functions on compact subsets of $\mathbb{R}^3$. This means that any continuous function can be approximated arbitrarily well by finite Gaussian expansions, and the multiscale structure gives a principled way to choose which scales to include.

## Where this representation shines in three dimensions

Several scientific domains rely on this representation:

- **Quantum chemistry** expresses atomic orbitals as linear combinations of Gaussian functions centered on nuclei, because Gaussian products are themselves Gaussian and integrals become analytically tractable. Contracted Gaussian basis sets are multiscale approximations to the cusped Slater-type orbitals.
- **Molecular and electron densities** are often modeled as sums of Gaussians at multiple widths, which captures diffuse valence regions and compact core regions in one representation.
- **Volumetric imaging and medical data** use Gaussian smoothing in scale-space analysis to detect blobs and edges at the scale where they are most stable, a technique formalized by Lindeberg.
- **Particle and meshfree methods** such as smoothed particle hydrodynamics and reproducing-kernel particle methods use Gaussian or Gaussian-like kernels to reconstruct fields from scattered samples, with the smoothing length acting as the scale.
- **Fast algorithms** exploit the kernel's structure. The Fast Gauss Transform evaluates sums of Gaussians at many points in near-linear time by grouping sources at the appropriate scale, making multiscale computation practical for large three-dimensional datasets.

## Limitations

The representation is powerful but not free. Gaussian smoothing is irreversible in practice: recovering $f$ from $f_\sigma$ is an ill-posed deconvolution, and high frequencies are damped by $\exp(-\sigma^2|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^2/2)$, so they are amplified enormously when inverted. This is why detail coefficients must be computed explicitly through differences rather than by inverting the blur. Interpolation with many narrow Gaussians also becomes ill-conditioned, since the kernel matrix approaches singularity as widths shrink relative to point spacing. Practitioners address this with regularization, multilevel preconditioning, or the flat-limit analysis of Driscoll and Fornberg. Finally, a Gaussian cannot represent a discontinuity sharply at any finite scale, so jumps require many levels of the decomposition to resolve, and the convergence rate is limited by the function's actual smoothness.

## Summary

The Gaussian kernel offers a single family that is smooth, localized in space and frequency, and self-consistent across scales. Its semigroup property makes coarse and fine approximations nest naturally, its heat-equation interpretation gives a physical picture of scale, and its separability in $\mathbb{R}^3$ keeps computation tractable. Multiscale difference decompositions turn this into an exact and controllable expansion, while Gaussian radial expansions provide flexible mesh-free approximations. Approximation error is governed by the regularity of the target function, which makes the representation both analyzable and honest about what it can resolve.
