# Solving the Hartree-Fock Equations with Numerical Atomic Orbitals in Non-Overlapping Atomic Spheres

## 1. Setting

The Hartree-Fock (HF) equations for a closed-shell system are

$$
\hat{F}\,\phi_i(\mathbf{r}) = \varepsilon_i\,\phi_i(\mathbf{r}), \qquad
\hat{F} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^2 + V_{\text{nuc}}(\mathbf{r}) + \hat{J}[\rho] - \hat{K},
$$

with density $\rho(\mathbf{r}) = 2\sum_i^{N_{\text{occ}}/2} |\phi_i(\mathbf{r})|^2$ and nonlocal exchange $\hat{K}$. The problem is nonlinear and is solved by self-consistent field (SCF) iteration in a finite basis, which yields the Roothaan-Hall generalised eigenproblem

$$
\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{C}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}.
$$

This document concerns a specific choice of basis: numerical atomic orbitals (NAOs) whose support is restricted to non-overlapping spheres centred on the atoms.

## 2. The geometric partition

Space is partitioned into

- atomic spheres $\Omega_\alpha = \{\mathbf{r} : |\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R} _ \alpha| < R _ \alpha\}$, with radii chosen so that $\Omega_\alpha \cap \Omega_\beta = \emptyset$ for $\alpha \neq \beta$, and
- an interstitial region $I = \mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \bigcup_\alpha \Omega_\alpha$.

Non-overlap is a strict geometric condition, $R _ \alpha + R _ \beta \leq |\mathbf{R} _ \alpha - \mathbf{R} _ \beta|$. It has an immediate consequence that shapes the whole method: a basis function confined to $\Omega_\alpha$ has zero overlap with any function confined to $\Omega_\beta$. Interatomic bonding therefore cannot be described by atom-centred functions alone, and the interstitial region must carry part of the basis. This is the central design issue, and the rest of the algorithm follows from how it is handled.

## 3. The basis

Inside each sphere, the basis consists of radial numerical functions multiplied by real spherical harmonics,

$$
\chi_{\alpha n l m}(\mathbf{r}) = R_{\alpha n l}(|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_\alpha|)\,Y_{lm}\!\left(\widehat{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_\alpha}\right), \qquad |\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_\alpha| < R_\alpha,
$$

where $R_{\alpha n l}$ are tabulated radial functions, typically generated by solving atomic-like problems with a confining potential that makes them vanish at $R_\alpha$, and $n$ labels the radial multiplicity (for example, single-zeta and double-zeta). Each $R_{\alpha n l}(r)$ is zero for $r \geq R_\alpha$, so the atom-centred functions are compactly supported.

The interstitial region needs a complementary representation that matches the sphere functions. Two common options are:

- Plane waves or a real-space grid, as in augmented-plane-wave-style schemes, where the interstitial part of the orbital is expanded freely and joined to the sphere solutions.
- Smooth continuation functions, such as spherical Bessel or Gaussian-like tails, defined in $I$ and matched to the sphere functions and their radial derivatives at $r = R_\alpha$.

Matching is what makes the combined basis variational. Without it, the discontinuity at sphere boundaries introduces spurious contributions to the energy. In a plane-wave-interstitial scheme the full basis is the union of sphere functions and interstitial plane waves, with linear combinations constrained so that the orbital and its normal derivative are continuous across each sphere boundary.

The overall orbital expansion is

$$
\phi_i(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{\alpha n l m} C^{\alpha n l m}_i\,\chi_{\alpha n l m}(\mathbf{r}) + \sum_{\mathbf{G}} C^{\mathbf{G}}_i\,e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,\Theta_I(\mathbf{r}),
$$

where $\Theta_I$ is the indicator of the interstitial region and the second sum is present only in the mixed scheme.

## 4. Matrix elements

Because sphere functions on different atoms do not overlap, the overlap and Hamiltonian matrices split naturally into blocks. Intra-sphere blocks are computed with radial quadrature and angular quadrature (for example, Lebedev grids), and the spherical-harmonic structure reduces the angular integrals to Gaunt-type coefficients. Inter-atomic blocks vanish for purely sphere-confined functions and are nonzero only through the interstitial part of the basis.

The overlap matrix has the form

$$
S_{\mu\nu} = \int_{\Omega_\alpha} \chi_\mu^\ast\chi_\nu\,d\mathbf{r} \;+\; \int_{\Omega_\beta} \chi_\mu^\ast\chi_\nu\,d\mathbf{r} \;+\; \int_{I} \chi_\mu^\ast\chi_\nu\,d\mathbf{r},
$$

where the three terms are evaluated over the appropriate regions. Core Hamiltonian elements are built the same way, with the kinetic operator and nuclear attraction treated per sphere and in $I$.

The nuclear attraction within a sphere is a standard one-centre problem with the potential of its own nucleus, plus a smooth contribution from neighbouring nuclei that can be expanded in spherical harmonics about $\mathbf{R}_\alpha$. Because the expansion is in $l$, its convergence is controlled by $l_{\max}$, which is a clean, explicit truncation parameter.

## 5. Coulomb potential

The Hartree potential decomposes naturally. Within each sphere, the density is expanded in spherical harmonics about the centre,

$$
\rho(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{lm} \rho_{\alpha,lm}(r)\,Y_{lm}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}), \qquad r < R_\alpha,
$$

and the Poisson equation $\nabla^2 V_H = -4\pi\rho$ reduces to a set of one-dimensional radial equations,

$$
\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dV_{H,lm}}{dr}\right) - \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}V_{H,lm} = -4\pi\rho_{lm}(r).
$$

These are solved with radial boundary conditions set by the multipole moments of the density outside the sphere and the contribution from the interstitial region. In the interstitial region, the potential is solved by Fourier transform on a grid or by a multigrid solver on a real-space mesh, matched to the sphere solutions on $\partial\Omega_\alpha$.

This split is the main computational advantage of the sphere partition: the dominant Coulomb work is one-dimensional radial solves per atom and per $(l,m)$ channel, which are cheap and accurate, with only the interstitial part requiring a multidimensional solve.

## 6. Exchange

The exchange operator is nonlocal, and its matrix elements $K_{\mu\nu} = \sum_\lambda\sigma P_{\lambda\sigma}(\mu\lambda|\nu\sigma)$ are the expensive part. The sphere partition helps in two ways. First, the two-electron integrals where all four functions lie on a single sphere reduce to one-centre quantities that can be computed with radial and angular quadrature without any cross-sphere coupling. Second, integrals involving functions on two or more different atoms are nonzero only when the interstitial part of the basis is involved, so the set of significant two-centre and three-centre integrals is much smaller than in a fully delocalised basis.

Practical implementations typically exploit this by:

- evaluating one-centre integrals exactly on sphere quadrature grids,
- restricting multi-centre integrals to pairs whose supports overlap through $I$, using a cutoff on the interstitial coefficient magnitude, and
- approximating the remaining multi-centre exchange through density fitting in an auxiliary basis, which is the usual route to $O(N^3)$ to $O(N^4)$ scaling in practice.

## 7. SCF algorithm

The iteration proceeds as follows.

```
1. Define atomic spheres (centres R_alpha, radii R_alpha with non-overlap check).
2. Generate radial NAOs R_{alpha n l} confined to each sphere, and the
   interstitial representation (plane waves, grid, or continuation functions).
3. Build S, H_core block by block (sphere blocks, interstitial block, cross terms).
4. Build orthogonaliser X = S^{-1/2} (canonical, dropping near-zero eigenvalues).
5. Initial density: superposition of atomic sphere densities, extended into I.
6. For n = 0, 1, 2, ...:
   a. Radial Poisson solves per sphere and (l,m) channel; interstitial Poisson solve.
   b. Exchange: one-centre integrals exactly; multi-centre via screened or fitted integrals.
   c. Form F^(n) = H_core + J - K (RHF).
   d. DIIS error  e = F P S - S P F  (in the orthogonal basis).
   e. DIIS extrapolation of F.
   f. Diagonalise X^T F X, recover C = X C'.
   g. Form P from the lowest N_occ/2 orbitals.
   h. E = 1/2 Tr[P (H_core + F)] + E_nuc.
   i. Converged when |dE| and ||e|| fall below tolerances; otherwise continue.
```

## 8. Numerical concerns specific to this scheme

Matching at sphere boundaries. The continuity of the orbital and its derivative across $\partial\Omega_\alpha$ must be enforced accurately. Residual mismatch appears as variational error that does not decrease with more basis functions, so it should be monitored directly.

Radius choice. Sphere radii must satisfy the non-overlap condition, and a larger radius improves the atomic description but reduces the interstitial region available for bonding. A standard test is to vary $R_\alpha$ and check the sensitivity of total energy and bond properties.

Angular convergence. Expansions in $l$ within spheres are truncated at $l_{\max}$. Convergence should be checked independently of the radial basis size, since the two parameters interact.

Core-valence separation. Deep core states may be treated fully inside spheres, which is natural, but their radial functions must be resolved on a fine radial grid near the nucleus.

Basis completeness. Completeness is shared between the radial NAO set and the interstitial representation. Improving only one of them does not necessarily improve the total energy; the two should be converged together.

## 9. Implementation notes for C++

- Separate the code into three modules: sphere (radial and angular quadrature, radial Poisson solves), interstitial (grid or plane-wave operations), and matching (boundary conditions and cross-region assembly). This keeps the matching logic testable on its own.
- Store radial functions on a common log-spaced grid per species so that sphere operations share data and cache behaviour is predictable.
- Use real spherical harmonic tables precomputed on Lebedev grids; the Gaunt coefficients are reused across all integrals.
- Parallelise over atoms for sphere work (embarrassingly parallel) and over grid blocks for the interstitial work, with a distributed dense solver for the diagonalisation.
- Validate first on a single atom in a sphere (no interstitial coupling), where the result should match a standard radial HF solver, then on a diatomic with known bond length.

## 10. Summary

Confining NAOs to non-overlapping atomic spheres turns the Hartree-Fock problem into a set of largely independent one-centre problems coupled through an interstitial region. The Coulomb potential reduces mostly to radial Poisson equations, exchange splits into one-centre integrals plus a smaller set of multi-centre terms, and the SCF loop is the standard Roothaan-Hall iteration with DIIS. The main difficulty is the matching between sphere and interstitial representations, which must be variationally consistent for the method to converge to the correct HF energy.
